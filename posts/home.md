# Journey from No Home to My Own Home

Since I was a kid, I always had one constant thought: I wanted a room for myself. At that time, it felt like the biggest thing I could ever achieve in life.

Throughout my life, we lived in rented houses. The struggles we faced were very real. Some were the usual problems everyone in a rental home deals with, like the lack of freedom or the constant moving, but others were deeply personal. To give some context, our family lived right on the line between the lower-middle class and poverty. My parents were daily-wage workers. We had no savings, no property, and nothing passed down to us.

On top of the financial stress, the environment at home was often difficult. Through all of that, it was mostly my mom who stood by me and supported my education. Being an only son, the weight of our situation was always on my mind.

**The Life of a Tenant**

We dealt with many different landlords over the years. By the 5th of every month, they would be standing at our door, asking for the rent. My mom would have to come up with new excuses every month just to buy us a little more time. We almost always had two or three months of rent piled up, struggling to catch up.

By the time I finished my undergraduate degree, we had moved about ten times. Out of all those places, only one was a proper 1BHK. The rest were just small 10x16 rooms, cramped and split into a tiny kitchen and a bedroom. In some houses, we had to share a single restroom with all the other tenants.

Fetching water was another constant struggle. Sometimes, the corporation water would only come in the middle of the night, and we’d have to wake up at 2:00 AM or 3:00 AM to get it. Other times, it came in the morning, and I’d have to miss the start of school just to help. If it came in the afternoon, my mom would have to leave her work just to run home. My job was always the same: filling up the big 200-liter drum and all the plastic water pots (*kudams*) we owned.

Then there was the issue of electricity. Most of the houses we lived in had one shared meter for all the neighbors. Since my father ran his sewing machines at home to earn our living, the neighbors would watch the meter and complain that our work was driving up their bills, leading to endless arguments.

Most of our houses had tiled roofs (*ottu veedu*) that leaked. When the rains were heavy, I couldn't even sit inside to do my schoolwork. The water would sprinkle down from the roof, wetting my books and my papers. We didn't even have a proper place to wash dishes; My mom would carry the vessels in front of the house with a bucket of water and sit on the floor to clean them.

Most of my friends in school and college lived in their own homes. I used to watch them and wonder what that felt like, to not be a tenant, to not have to move, and to have a room just for myself.

**Building the Dream**

That curiosity stayed with me for 25 years. Every struggle pushed me to start planning for a house the moment I began earning. After working for about two years, my mom and I started looking for plots. We eventually purchased a piece of land in the countryside—my very first non-movable asset.

Buying the land drained all our savings, so we had to wait another two years to fill our accounts back up. By then, my plans were getting complicated. I wasn't sure where I would eventually settle, since my work is always in Tier-1 cities. We even explored selling the land to buy a pre-owned house elsewhere, but luck wasn’t on our side. Finally, we decided: we would build on our own plot.

I sat down with my cousin, who was just starting his career as a structural engineer. At first, we realized the plot was only big enough for a basic 2BHK. But my view was different: we are building a house only once in a lifetime. It should be enough for at least two generations. After crushing and tossing away multiple papers, we settled on a 3BHK duplex plan.

Some people insisted that I should only build the ground floor and leave the terrace to build a rental portion later. That would have meant a smaller hall, kitchen, and bedrooms. After 25 years of living in cramped rentals, my heart wouldn't accept that. I didn't want to compromise on my own space just to become a landlord.

The plan was finalized, and we laid the first brick. At that moment, I realized only two things stood between me and my dream: an enormous amount of time and a huge financial debt. The excitement of the child in me was slowly replaced by the heavy responsibility of the adult I had become.

Fortunately, my job at the time was fully remote, so I could visit the site often and support my cousin. But halfway through the construction, the pressure of the loan started to hit me. I realized that while I was achieving my childhood dream, I was also stepping into a very difficult financial chapter.

After months of hard work and a mountain of debt, the house is finally built. Thanks to my cousin, who cared about every corner of the plan more than anyone, the home turned out to be everything I ever hoped for. But as the house was completed, I reached a crossroads. I knew that if I wanted to grow in my career and reach the next level, I couldn't stay. To fuel that growth, I had to move to a Tier-1 city.

So, while my mom and grandma finally moved into our new home, I moved to a new city for a new job. I spent so long dreaming of this space, but now that it's ready, my career has taken me to a new city. I won't be living here every day, but I will be traveling back often to visit them and the home I built. It was hard at first, accepting that I wouldn’t be living full-time in the house I built for us. But I knew that staying in a Tier-1 city was the only way to fuel my career growth, so I moved to Bangalore.

In our society, as soon as a young man builds a house, the next expectation is always marriage. I didn't want an arranged marriage, so moving to a new city gave me the chance to find someone myself and I did. Her family is settled here in Bangalore, and after dating for a year, we are planning our marriage with both families' blessings. Because she works in healthcare, she isn't able to travel often. This means I’m the one who will be traveling back frequently to visit my family and the home.

I’m in a bittersweet place right now: happy that I found "the one," but sad that I’ll spend less time with my mom and grandma. I’m still figuring out how to manage this new balance.

**The House We Built:**

The house is finally standing. It has a big hall and a large kitchen designed specifically for my mom. The layout includes one bedroom on the ground floor, while the first floor holds the master bedroom and a multi-purpose room that opens up to a beautiful view from the balcony.

My cousin insisted we never compromise on quality, so we used high-grade, long-lasting materials for every part of the build. We ensured there was proper ventilation in every room and kept the interiors simple, focusing only on what was absolutely necessary. Now, I’m planning a garden on the balcony for my mom and grandma so they can grow their own vegetables and be self-sustained.

During those first few nights, I couldn't sleep. I was overthinking every decision and every rupee spent. But slowly, my mind came to terms with it. All the effort and the debt felt worth it the moment I saw my mom and grandma sitting in their own space. For the first time in my life, they don't have to answer to a landlord at the beginning of the month. The building is finished, and now my mom and grandma are doing the real work of turning it into a home.

This journey taught me that we often carry unfulfilled dreams for a long time. By the time we are capable of achieving them, we are different people in different places.

But I realized this wasn't just my dream; it was a shared one. My perspective might have changed as I grew up, but the women who took care of me never lost sight of what they needed. They deserve this more than I do. I’ve made some trade-offs like living in a different city and carrying the weight of debt, but seeing them at peace makes it worth it. I want to grow, but I never want to leave behind the ones who raised me.
